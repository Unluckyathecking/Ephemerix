How far SPHEREx could see each kind of planet

This is our estimate of how far SPHEREx could see a distant planet, not a detection and not a search result.

Each bar runs from a small, dark version of the planet (left end) to a large, bright one (right end); the dot is the most likely case. The amber band is where Planet Nine is predicted.

Why we claim no Planet X

One pass of our search cannot find Planet Nine. One SPHEREx map sees a 10-Earth-mass planet only to about 375 au (225 to 550), short of most of the predicted range, and between passes six months apart it jumps 7 to 25 arcminutes, out of any small field. Finding it would take linking three or four detections from separate passes across the whole sky, which we have not built.

We claim no Planet X.

How we worked this out

How bright would Planet Nine be?

One proposed orbit has a semimajor axis of 400 to 800 astronomical units (au) for a planet with 5 to 10 times Earth’s mass (Batygin et al. 2019). A later fit favours about 6 Earth masses and 380 au, with a range of 300 to 520 au (Brown & Batygin 2021). One au is the average distance from Earth to the Sun. In reflected sunlight such a planet would be about AB 20 to 22 in the SPHEREx bands at 500 au and fade by 3 magnitudes every time its distance doubles.

Brightness here is in magnitudes (AB and V are two versions of the scale): a bigger number is fainter, and 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100. "5 sigma" means the planet stands out at five times the noise.

For the 10-Earth-mass model, our estimate for one spectral channel in a single SPHEREx sky map is about 375 au (225 to 550). Averaging all 17 channels of one detector on the moving planet would stretch that to about 550 au (350 to 800). Using the formal per-frame errors of our own SPHEREx photometry instead, the figure is about 325 au.

A planet could also glow from its own heat. A planet at the top of the mass range with a methane-depleted atmosphere could be as bright as W1 16 near 3.3 µm (Fortney et al. 2016). WISE already excludes that case inside about 820 au, and a single SPHEREx pass at 2.4 to 3.8 µm would see it out to about 1070 au. By our own estimate this 820 to 1070 au window shrinks or closes once a blind search needs about 7 sigma and has to link three or four detections that jump about 7 arcminutes each time.

Brightness against distance, band by band

How to read this plot

Each coloured line is one hypothetical planet with its most likely size, albedo and temperature; the shaded band around it runs from the faintest to the brightest plausible version. Brighter is up. The flat grey bands are survey depths at 5 sigma: a planet above a band would be detected, one below it would not. The amber strip marks 400 to 800 au, where the Planet Nine hypothesis puts the planet.

Reflected light falls as distance to the fourth power. Heat from the planet's interior falls only as distance squared, which is why the methane-depleted WISE models (shown only for W1 and W2) are nearly flat.

Depths marked "derived" are scaled from the published two-year SPHEREx depth (Bock et al. 2025) by us; "measured" is the per-frame noise of our own photometry, which is a formal error and probably 0.75 to 1.5 magnitudes too optimistic.

How it would move

SPHEREx looks about 90 degrees from the Sun, where Earth's offset of 1 au is across the line of sight. Six months later Earth is on the other side, so a distant planet jumps by twice its parallax between passes. Within one pass it creeps by Earth's sideways speed, which is zero in the ecliptic and largest at the poles.

The numbers

How far each survey reaches

Farthest distance at which the planet is still above the 5-sigma depth, best band, most likely value and range.

The Jupiter analogue is drawn as a 100 K blackbody, which badly underestimates the 4 to 5 µm light of a real giant planet, so its WISE and long-wavelength SPHEREx figures are lower bounds; WISE rules out Saturn and Jupiter analogues out to 28,000 and 82,000 au (Luhman 2014).

How far it would move

Planet's own motion assumes a circular orbit seen side on (an upper bound). One SPHEREx pixel is 6.15 arcsec. Planet Nine's predicted 15 to 25 degree inclination keeps it within about 25 degrees of the ecliptic.

What earlier searches have ruled out

Anything SPHEREx could see in reflected light would be brighter than V 21. The Pan-STARRS search alone is half complete at V 21.5, and together with ZTF and DES it has ruled out 78% of the favoured orbits without finding the planet (Brown, Holman & Batygin 2024). WISE has ruled out the brightest models that glow in their own heat at 3 to 5 µm (W1 < 16.7 over three quarters of the sky), and the far-infrared IRAS and AKARI searches have only unconfirmed candidates. A planet could still be farther away, fainter, or hidden among the stars of the Galactic plane. Each would make a detection harder.

What could be wrong

Ranges run from a small, dark planet against the shallow end of the survey depth to a large, bright one against the deep end. The bright end assumes a grey albedo of 0.75; methane makes real ice giants much darker at 1 to 5 µm, so the true reach is probably nearer the low end.
